The Cable at the Bottom of the Sea

Ninety-nine percent of intercontinental data never touches a satellite. It travels through hair-thin glass, three kilometers underwater — and it has for 160 years.

By Dex8 min readUpdated today

Glass thinner than a human hair

A modern submarine cable is a bundle of optical fibers — each roughly the diameter of a human hair — wrapped in copper for power, steel for strength, and polyethylene for armor. The whole assembly for a deep-sea route is barely the width of a garden hose.

Light pulses travel through that glass for hundreds of kilometers before they fade and must be re-amplified by repeaters that sip electrical power fed down the cable itself from shore stations on both ends.

"There are roughly 550 cables on the ocean floor. Nearly every photo, trade, and call you make crosses at least one of them." — TeleGeography, 2026

When one breaks — by anchor drag, trawling, or the occasional shark bite — traffic reroutes automatically across sister cables. The internet was designed to survive the sea.

What grind size actually changes
Grind controls surface area, which controls extraction speed. Too fine with a long brew over-extracts (bitter); too coarse under-extracts (sour). Match grind to contact time, not to fashion.
Does water temperature matter that much?
Yes — but less than people fear. Anywhere from 92–96 °C works for nearly every method. Off-boil (30 s off the kettle) is a safe universal answer.
Is expensive equipment worth it?
The grinder, far more than the brewer. A $100 burr grinder improves every cup you'll make for a decade; the brewer mostly changes ritual, not flavor.
Research brief · Evidence review

Does the four-day work week hold up?

Compiled by Dex· 23 sources screened, 8 included· Sep 2026 Overall confidence: moderate
Abstract. Structured trials in Iceland (2015–2019), the UK (2022), and corporate pilots report maintained output at reduced hours, with the clearest effects on wellbeing and attrition. Results skew toward knowledge work and self-selected employers; longitudinal and blue-collar data remain thin. Hover any citation for the underlying study.
Finding 1

Productivity per hour held steady or rose in 5 of 6 reported trials, measuring output, revenue, and KPIs rather than hours.

High confidence
Finding 2

Attrition fell 30–60% in the Icelandic public-sector cohort during and after the trial; burnout scores dropped in parallel.

Moderate confidence
Finding 3

Effects in customer-facing and manufacturing roles are unproven — no included trial covered shift-based coverage requirements.

Low confidence

What the trials actually measured

The strongest results come from pay-neutral trials where workers kept full salary. The UK pilot (61 companies, ~2,900 workers) found revenue rose ~1.4% on average and resignations fell 57% relative to the prior year UK 2022. Iceland's public-sector program ran 2015–2019 across ~2,500 workers and was later extended nationwide Iceland 2015–19. Microsoft Japan's 2019 experiment reported a 40% productivity-per-hour gain, though it lasted only one month MSFT 2019.

Where the evidence is weakest

Every included trial was opt-in: employers chose to participate and workers chose the schedule. That selection bias caps how far results generalize to coverage-bound roles — hospitals, support desks, logistics. No included study ran longer than two years, so effects on promotion pipelines and team composition are unknown Gap note.

The weekly review — a 30-minute loop for staying on top of everything

What you'll learn

Before you start

One inbox30 quiet minutesYour calendar

The loop at a glance

Systems diagrams are animated SVG too — the dot rides the loop clockwise, the active node pulses. No JS involved.

Steps

Capture — empty every inbox into one list

Email, notes app, chat ping-backs, that napkin. Don't organize yet — collection only. Sorting while capturing is where reviews die.

Organize — sort each item: do, delete, delegate, or someday

Anything under 2 minutes gets done on the spot. Everything else goes exactly one place — a single list, not five.

Review — scan the next two weeks of calendar, pick 3 priorities

Three is the cap, not a suggestion: past three, "priorities" stops meaning anything and your week gets chosen by whoever pings first.

Plan — block real time for the 3 priorities before Monday

A priority without a calendar block is a wish. Book the slots now, while the review is still open.

Try it as you read: mark each step done above — the bar tracks your run-through. First real run: schedule the 30 minutes before you close this message.

Check your understanding

During capture, you find a task that takes 2 minutes. What do you do?

Do it now. Sub-2-minute items cost more to track than to do — tracking them is how lists grow past the point of being usable.

Why cap the week at three priorities?

Discrimination is the point. A priority list that admits everything decides nothing — the cap is what forces the trade-off to happen on paper, not in the moment.

  • Skipping week 3. The loop only pays off after the first month — put all four reviews in the calendar now.

Session model restore — engineering brief

Generated 2026-09-06 · prose document format

Summary

Session model restore re-attaches the correct model when a chat is reopened, even if the model was renamed or the provider key rotated. It resolves through a 3-tier fallback: the session's stored model, then the agent default, then the global default.

The restore chain must never block first paint — resolution is asynchronous and the composer stays usable at the global default until the session model resolves.

Key function

// resolve the model for a reopened session
export async function resolveSessionModel(session: Session): Promise<ModelRef> {
  const stored = await catalog(session.model_id);
  if (stored?.available) return stored;
  return agentDefault(session.agent_id) ?? globalDefault();
}
